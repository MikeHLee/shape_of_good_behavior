## Post 1 — hook  📎 fig1_exploit_resistance.png

Human preference data has loops: graders say A beats B, B beats C… and C
beats A.

Reward models trained on loopy data develop exploitable soft spots.

We taught 3 training methods to see the loops first. Biggest jump: 80.0% →
99.6% exploit resistance, across all 30 seeds. 🧵

> **Alt text**: Bar chart titled "Reward optimizers that can see preference
> loops leave far fewer exploitable rankings." Exploit resistance, mean of 30
> seeds: KTO 80.0% vs Hodge-KTO 99.64% (+19.6 points, d=16.47, p<0.0001);
> DPO 94.0% vs Hodge-DPO 99.99% (+6.0 points, d=6.52, p<0.0001); GRPO and
> Hodge-GRPO both already at the 100% metric ceiling.

## Post 2 — the setup

Quick setup. AI assistants are trained on comparisons: show graders two
answers, record which one is better. A reward model learns to score answers
from those comparisons, and the AI is then trained to chase that score.

The catch: the comparisons don't always add up.

## Post 3 — what a loop is  📎 fig2_preference_loop.png

Rock–paper–scissors preferences are real: A beats B (more thorough), B
beats C (clearer), C beats A (more concise).

Each judgment is locally reasonable. Together they form a loop — and a
score can't represent a loop. It's an Escher staircase.

> **Alt text**: Concept diagram titled "A single score per answer cannot
> represent a loop of preferences." Left: three answer boxes with arrows
> A beats B, B beats C, C beats A, captioned "an Escher staircase: every step
> goes up, yet you end where you started." Right: the Hodge split — a
> "rankable part" (A above B above C, which a score can fit) plus a "pure
> loop" (which no score fits), with the note that unflagged loops are where
> reward models grow exploitable soft spots.

## Post 4 — why loops hurt

Forced to fit a loop, the reward model compromises — somewhere it scores an
answer it shouldn't prefer above one it should.

Those compromises are exactly what RL training finds. The model isn't
optimizing "be good"; it's optimizing "score high," soft spots included.

## Post 5 — the tool

There's century-old math for this: Hodge decomposition.

Feed it your comparisons and it splits them into
• a consistent ranking — the part a score CAN represent
• pure loops — the part it can't

Like fitting an altitude map to "X is uphill of Y" claims. The leftover is
Escher.

## Post 6 — the method

Our fix: while training the reward model, add a gentle pull toward the
loop-free ranking, and down-weight comparisons that sit deep inside loops.

We call these Hodge-aware variants — Hodge-DPO, Hodge-KTO, Hodge-GRPO. Same
optimizers, plus a loop map.

## Post 7 — the numbers  📎 fig3_paired_seeds.png

Results, 30 seeds on 500 preference pairs (+1,482 graph edges):

• KTO 80.0% → Hodge-KTO 99.6% (d=16.47, p<0.0001)
• DPO 94.0% → Hodge-DPO 99.99% (d=6.52, p<0.0001)

Not an average hiding variance: every single seed improved, in both
families.

> **Alt text**: Dot plot titled "Not an average hiding variance: all 30 seeds
> improved, in both families." Per-seed improvement in exploit resistance,
> Hodge variant minus standard. KTO family: mean +19.6 percentage points,
> worst seed +15.8. DPO family: mean +6.0, worst seed +3.8. A dashed
> "no change" line at zero has every dot well to its right.

## Post 8 — what didn't change

Honest nulls: GRPO was already at 100% on this benchmark — a ceiling. No
method can improve on a saturated metric, so "Hodge-GRPO = GRPO" is
uninformative, not a win.

And ORPO (no Hodge variant tested) sat at 62.2% — the spread between
standard methods is real.

## Post 9 — the honest caveats (LONG)

Caveats, because they matter:

• This is an embedding-level benchmark: small scorer networks over frozen
sentence embeddings, ranking a fixed list of (ideal, exploit) response
pairs. No text generation, no RL optimization pressure — "exploit
resistance" here is ranking accuracy, a proxy.

• Two things changed together in the version that works (the regularizer
AND the preference-graph construction). The clean ablation separating them
hasn't been run.

• When we replayed the idea at 1.5B scale with real PPO, the margin shrank
to ~2 points at n=51 — not statistically significant. That war story is
thread 3.

## Post 10 — credit where due (LONG)

Lineage, because none of this came from nowhere:

• The math: HodgeRank — Jiang, Lim, Yao, Ye (2011)
• Cycles provably break scalar reward models: Liu, Long, Shi, Su & Xiao
(Annals of Statistics)
• Alignment beyond one scalar reward: Nash-LHF (Munos et al.), SPO
(@g_k_swamy et al.), SPPO, GPM
• Closest neighbor: "Transitivity Meets Cyclicity" (ICML 2026) also splits
preferences into transitive + cyclic parts — via game theory. Our delta:
combinatorial Hodge machinery on the preference graph itself, plus a
reward-hacking evaluation.

## Post 11 — the deepest caveat + links

Deepest caveat: the geometry inherits its inputs. Loop cleanup makes a
reward model faithful to the consistent core of the preferences you
collected. If they encode the wrong values, you get a very robust wrong
thing.

Code + data: github.com/MikeHLee/shape_of_good_behavior
