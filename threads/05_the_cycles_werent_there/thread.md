## Post 1 — hook

We spent months building preference optimization that respects cycles in human
feedback — the A>B>C>A loops a single reward score can't represent.

Then we computed one number we should have computed first.

There were no cycles. 🧵

## Post 2 — the idea, honestly stated

The theory is sound and still is: RLHF collapses feedback to one scalar. A scalar
reward's pairwise flow is s(a)−s(b) — a gradient — which is mathematically
incapable of encoding A>B>C>A.

So cyclic preference is invisible to it. Real gap.

## Post 3 — what we built

Hodge decomposition splits a preference graph into a transitive part and a cyclic
part. We trained on the cycle-free component (Hodge-DPO, Hodge-KTO) and got
in-sample ranking accuracy of 0.9999 vs 0.940.

We were pleased. That was the problem.

## Post 4 — first correction

Held out, it vanished: Hodge-DPO 0.537 vs DPO 0.550. Hodge-KTO 0.543 vs KTO 0.542.
Split-level p = 0.49 (DPO) and 0.97 (KTO), 5 splits × 30 seeds.

A margin control — same loss, every target replaced by the mean — reproduced the
entire in-sample gain. It was never cycle-aware.

## Post 5 — the number we should have computed first

A graph can only hold cycles if its first Betti number b₁ = E − V + C is > 0.

Our label graph: 500 labels, 998 responses, 498 components.

b₁ = 500 − 998 + 498 = **0**

## Post 6 — what b₁ = 0 means  📎 fig1_label_graph.png

Zero isn't "few cycles." It's a forest — 497 disjoint edges and one 3-star.

On a forest, cyclic energy is **exactly zero for every possible labelling**. Not
just ours. No labelling of that dataset could have shown intransitivity.

> **Alt text**: Two panels. Left, "The labels we collected": 498 tiny disconnected
> components, nearly all single edges joining two responses, annotated b₁ = 0 —
> no cycles possible. Right, "The graph we analyzed": the same 400 label edges plus
> 3,998 kNN similarity edges densely interconnecting everything, annotated 90.9%
> synthetic, h₁ ≈ 2.22.

## Post 7 — so where did our cycles come from?

We'd augmented the graph with k-nearest-neighbour edges from embedding similarity.
400 real labels, 3,998 synthetic edges — 90.9%.

Every cycle we decomposed ran through at least one invented edge.

We measured our own preprocessing.

## Post 8 — why the data can't cycle, structurally

It's not a preprocessing bug. It's collection design.

HH-RLHF gives one (chosen, rejected) pair per prompt. Each response appears in
exactly one comparison. Mean degree 1.002.

Intransitivity needs an item compared against *several* alternatives. Ours never was.

## Post 9 — this explains the null (LONG)

Our September re-test showed Hodge-DPO ≈ DPO but couldn't say why. Tuning?
Regularizer? More seeds?

b₁ = 0 closes it. There was no structure to exploit, so the null was forced by the
data's design — not by method configuration.

An explained null is worth more than an unexplained one. It tells you what to change
instead of what to retry.

## Post 10 — and then the metric betrayed us too

We assumed "cyclic energy > 0" meant genuine value tension. We checked.

A perfectly transitive scalar reward, collapsed to a **binary** label, shows 15.9%
cyclic energy and **zero** cycles.

Binarizing invents the signal.

## Post 11 — why that happens

Binary labels emit constant magnitude on every edge. Gradient flows must add along
paths; constant ones can't.

R0→R3→R6 sums to 0.00 while R0→R6 reads 2.20. Mismatch → "cyclic energy."

The ordering was consistent the whole time.

## Post 12 — the fix

Cyclic energy conflates real intransitivity, threshold artefacts, and vote noise.

Cycle **count** doesn't — it's ordinal, so monotone rescaling can't fake it.

New rule: cycle count first, with a permutation null. Energy only as a secondary
magnitude.

## Post 13 — the noise floor nobody budgets for

Even with zero true cyclicity, finite votes create some.

How much depends on how close the answers are. Well-separated answers, 100 votes/edge:
1.6% spurious. Near-tied answers, same budget: 40%.

There's no universal floor. Compute the null from your own margins.

## Post 14 — what actually would work

Cycles need a decision rule whose *dominant principle changes with the pair*.

Tversky's semiorder: decide on helpfulness if the gap clears ε, else fall to
harmlessness. Small gaps chain into a big one → A>B>C>A.

30 of 200 random item sets cycled at ε=1.5.

## Post 15 — relative isn't enough

A relative ranking rule that's monotone in a *fixed* principle blend: 0 of 200 sets
cycled. Transitive.

Threshold-free relativity buys nothing. The discontinuity is the mechanism, not
relativity itself.

Precision matters here.

## Post 16 — the design that follows

Cycles are local to a prompt. With k responses, all pairs compared, b₁ =
(k−1)(k−2)/2.

k=2 → 0. k=3 → 1. k=5 → 6 cycles for 10 comparisons.

You don't need global n² pairing. You need triangles. Budget is O(k²) per prompt.

## Post 17 — the trap we're now watching for

We can generate cycles synthetically with a semiorder grader. Tempting: cheap
"intransitive" data.

That's our kNN mistake in new clothes — manufacturing the phenomenon.

Synthetic cycles are a **positive control** only. Never evidence about people.

## Post 18 — what we still don't know

Whether human preferences actually contain cycles at usable density is **open**. Our
data couldn't answer it.

Odds a cycle-aware method beats a strong scalar baseline once the data exists: we'd
say ~15%. The theory is clean; the payoff isn't established.

## Post 19 — closing

The maths was never wrong. A scalar reward genuinely cannot represent cyclic
preference.

We just never checked whether our data had any — and our metric would have said yes
regardless.

Compute the Betti number of your raw observation graph. Before the method. 🧵/
